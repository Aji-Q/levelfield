# Research memo: what judges reward in infra/dev-tooling hackathon tracks

Date: 2026-08-20
Scope: Somnia × DreamDEX Event Contracts Hackathon (DoraHacks BUIDL, 2026-08-25 → 2026-09-08, $5k pool, Somnia Shannon testnet). Web research only, no repo access assumed for sourcing.

Bottom line up front: no public DoraHacks page, blog post, or announcement for the specific "Somnia × DreamDEX Event Contracts Hackathon" was found in indexed search as of 2026-08-20 — it is either not yet crawled or announced only via Discord/X, five days before the stated window opens. Every claim below about *this* hackathon's rubric is therefore inference from Somnia's and DreamDEX's general pattern (past mini-hackathons, grant programs, product docs), not a confirmed rubric for this specific event. That gap is flagged inline wherever it matters.

## 1. Somnia ecosystem: past hackathons, grants, DreamDEX-specific signal

1. Source: [Somnia DeFi Mini Hackathon](https://dorahacks.io/hackathon/defi-mini-hackathon/hackers), [Somnia Data Streams Mini Hackathon](https://dorahacks.io/hackathon/somnia-datastreams/detail), [Somnia Reactivity Hackathon recap](https://blog.somnia.network/p/somnia-reactivity-hackathon-shows).
2. The Data Streams Mini Hackathon's published evaluation axes are the most concrete rubric Somnia has stated publicly for a small/infra-flavored event: **technical implementation, effective use of real-time data, integration with Somnia, and potential to evolve into a production application or meaningful ecosystem contribution**. Prizes were flat ($300 in SOMI per winner), not tiered by track. Source: [Developers Experiment With Somnia Data Streams](https://blog.somnia.network/p/developers-experiment-with-somnia).
3. The Reactivity Hackathon recap explicitly showcased two infra/tooling projects with no consumer UI — Streamline (a no-code Reactivity workflow builder that compiles to Solidity) and Mesh (a reactive workflow engine using JSON DAGs for multi-contract automation) — alongside games and DeFi apps, confirming Somnia curators do feature and describe non-flashy infra builds in their public recap, not just consumer dapps. Source: [Somnia Reactivity Hackathon Shows Real-Time Onchain Applications](https://blog.somnia.network/p/somnia-reactivity-hackathon-shows).
4. DreamDEX is explicitly built "agent-first": native MCP server access, CCXT-compatible bindings, REST/WebSocket APIs, and native `AGENTS.md`/`SKILL.md` integration are called out as core architecture, not bolt-ons, in both the product blog post and the live site. Source: [dreamDEX is the endgame DEX](https://blog.somnia.network/p/dreamdex-is-the-endgame-dex), [dreamdex.io](https://www.dreamdex.io/). This is the single strongest piece of DreamDEX-specific signal for an MCP-server submission: the sponsor's own positioning treats agent/MCP tooling as a first-class use case, not a stretch fit.
5. Somnia's broader grant vehicle (Dreamathon, $200k) states selection criteria of "proven technical skills and a track record of delivery, clear well-defined use cases in high-impact categories, and evidence of user demand or early traction," evaluated across three milestones (MVP spec → mainnet MVP → growth metrics). Prediction Markets is explicitly one of the five target build categories. Source: [$200K in Grants... Somnia's Dreamathon](https://blog.somnia.network/p/200k-in-grants-up-for-grabs-in-somnias). (inference) This suggests Somnia-adjacent judges generally reward "traction/evidence" framing over pure feature lists, consistent with hackathon patterns elsewhere.
6. No DreamDEX team statement (blog, docs, or press) was found that names specific desired hackathon submissions (e.g., "we want a risk layer" or "we want an MCP wrapper"). The closest is the architecture description in point 4 — DreamDEX describes itself as built for "bots" and "autonomous agents" as first-class users, with no rate limits for algorithmic strategies. Source: [dreamdex.io](https://www.dreamdex.io/).

**Somnia/DreamDEX-specific evidence is thin** for this exact hackathon's rubric — everything above is extrapolated from sibling events and product docs, not a confirmed brief for the Event Contracts Hackathon itself.

## 2. Infra/tooling track patterns: substituting for the "wow" moment

1. ETHGlobal's general track criteria (seen consistently across event prize pages) are **Technicality, Originality, Practicality, Usability (UI/UX/DX), and "WOW Factor"** — the last one explicitly framed as "the catch-all for anything unique or impressive," i.e. it does not require a visual wow, but *something* has to be memorable. Source: [ETHGlobal Agentic Ethereum details](https://ethglobal.com/events/agents/info/details).
2. A recurring, independently-sourced finding across multiple hackathon-advice writeups: **winning demos show one workflow that works flawlessly end-to-end; losing demos show five half-working features and ask judges to "imagine if."** Source: [What 8,200 hackathon projects reveal about what actually wins](https://simbro.medium.com/what-8-200-hackathon-projects-reveal-about-what-actually-wins-f105346ec97c) (via search-result summary; full article behind a 403 paywall-style block, so treat as a secondary citation).
3. JetBrains' "notes from the judging table" is blunt on this exact tension: **"a strong project with a confusing demo loses to a simpler project that the judges understand."** Their prescribed structure for any project, UI or not, is to "put the judge in the shoes of the user and walk them through what happens" — i.e., for an infra project, the "user" can be a developer or an agent, but the narrative must still be concrete and walked through, not described abstractly. Source: [How to Win a Hackathon: Notes From the Judging Table](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/).
4. Same source's failure-mode list, directly applicable to an infra/SDK submission: skipping problem definition (assuming the pain point is self-evident), scope creep (5 features vs. 1 clean flow), building around "the coolest new tool" instead of the actual pain point, and unpolished demos (live API calls that stall instead of pre-filled/mocked data for anything non-core). Source: same as #3.
5. Real precedent for infra-without-UI winning at scale: the Solo.io "2026 Hackathon for MCP & AI Agents" gave dedicated tracks to pure-infra, no-consumer-UI submissions — a Kubernetes-native multi-cluster **inventory controller** for AI infra won its track for "strong thinking around scalable AI infrastructure management," and a **security-scoring and risk-analysis governance agent** for MCP resources won the "Secure & Govern MCP" track. Neither has a flashy frontend; both won on architecture clarity and a working control-plane demo. Source: [Celebrating the Winners of the 2026 Hackathon for MCP & AI Agents](https://www.solo.io/blog/celebrating-the-winners-of-the-2026-hackathon-for-mcp-ai-agents).
6. (inference) Across every rubric found (ETHGlobal, TAIKAI's 6-criteria framework, JetBrains), "impact/adoption potential" is scored separately from "wow" — an infra project can max the former even with a minimal or CLI-only demo, provided the walkthrough is concrete rather than a slide describing what the tool *could* do.

## 3. Validation credibility: presenting benchmarks without overclaiming

1. MLCommons' criteria for a trustworthy benchmark, distilled: explicit scope ("know what decision it informs and doesn't claim more than it measures"), documented data provenance and contamination controls, **reproducibility via versioned data/code/config/prompts/seeds that an independent party can reconstruct**, integrity controls (blinding, held-out items, validation of any LLM-judge against human ground truth), transparent limitations (multiple metrics, confidence intervals, explicit "what isn't measured" statements), and active maintenance. Source: [How to Tell When a Benchmark Is Worth Trusting](https://mlcommons.org/2026/08/benchmark-is-worth-trusting/).
2. The inverse — what makes judges/readers distrust a benchmark — per the same source and a secondary AI-benchmark-critique piece: single optimized metric hiding tradeoffs, undisclosed test conditions or inaccessible methodology, no contamination/leakage controls, and results "used beyond their intended scope" ("benchmark washing"). Source: same as #1; corroborating context from [Why Benchmarks Lie More Than Marketing Claims](https://jakubjirak.medium.com/why-benchmarks-lie-more-than-marketing-claims-af52ed5323a5) (secondary, not independently verified in full).
3. A specific, generalizable psychological trap flagged in search results: **"precision bias"** — a falsely-precise number ("twelve percent faster") reads as more credible than an honest qualitative statement, even when the qualitative one is the better predictor of real performance. The corrective is not to avoid numbers but to pair every number with its sample size, method, and what it does *not* cover. (inference on how to apply this to a hackathon submission, grounded in the sourced principle.)
4. No hackathon-specific rubric was found that explicitly scores "benchmark rigor" as a line item (ETHGlobal, TAIKAI, and JetBrains rubrics all omit it). (inference) This means validation numbers likely function less as a scored criterion and more as a *credibility multiplier* on "Technicality"/"Practicality" scores — i.e., a small, honestly-scoped, reproducible number (e.g., "3 blind runs, 16/16 band agreement, Spearman ρ=0.930 on a 16-contract set — full corpus and run scripts in `data/`") is more persuasive to a judge doing a 5-minute review than a larger but unverifiable claim, because it survives the "can I check this myself" instinct JetBrains' judges describe.
5. "Run it yourself" affordances (a script or command judges can execute) did not surface a dedicated hackathon-specific source, but is consistent with (a) reproducibility requirements in the MLCommons framework and (b) the "put the judge in the shoes of the user" principle from JetBrains — the strongest form of that principle for an infra project is a runnable command, not a described one. (inference, but grounded in two independent sourced principles converging on the same conclusion.)

## 4. Sponsor-feedback deliverables: what format/depth wins

1. Ledger's ETHGlobal prize track (Cannes and other events) runs a **dedicated Documentation Improvement track** whose stated qualification criteria are: "detailed analysis of documentation gaps," "suggestions for improvements with concrete examples," "identification of unclear or missing information," "ideas for better code examples or tutorials," and "UX/navigation improvement suggestions." Source: [ETHGlobal Cannes — Ledger prize page](https://ethglobal.com/events/cannes/prizes/ledger). No formal numeric rubric is published beyond this list — it reads as a checklist judges apply, not weighted scoring.
2. Hack the North 2025 ran a **standalone $250 bounty specifically for "best developer feedback"** on top of a sponsor's regular track prizes, confirming feedback-quality is sometimes rewarded as its own line item distinct from the technical build. Source: search-result summary of [Hack the North 2025 sponsor tracks](https://hackthenorth2025.devpost.com/) (secondary; page not independently fetched in full).
3. (inference, pattern-matched from #1) The Ledger checklist implies the winning format for an SDK/docs feedback report is: **concrete gaps** (not "docs could be better" but "field X is undocumented, here's the trace"), **suggested fix with example** (not just "add an example" but the example itself), and **severity/navigation framing** (what a new integrator would hit first). This maps directly onto a "Gotcha #N" style catalogue rather than prose commentary.
4. No source directly addressed *reproducibility of the feedback itself* (e.g., "here's the exact call that produced this error"), but this follows from the same reproducibility principle in section 3 — a feedback report that includes the exact failing request/response is categorically stronger evidence than a described issue, for the same "can I check this" reason judges reward in benchmarks.
5. No Somnia- or DreamDEX-specific SDK-feedback deliverable examples were found (no past Somnia hackathon publicly required or showcased an SDK feedback report as a submission artifact in the sources reviewed) — this appears to be specific to this hackathon's stated requirements per the project brief, not a pattern inherited from Somnia's hackathon history.

## 5. Agent/MCP + on-chain protocol: convincing demo patterns, and direct precedent

1. **Closest direct precedent found**: BNB Chain's "Good Vibes Only: OpenClaw Edition" hackathon ($100k pool, 300 submissions, 600 hackers, scored 60% expert judging / 40% community vote) placed three risk/attestation/agent-monitoring projects in its top 10 — almost exactly the shape of a risk-scoring + MCP + attestation submission:
   - **VibeCheck** (8th place): paste a token address, get an AI-generated safety score backed by **immutable onchain attestation on opBNB**. Framed by BNB Chain as filling a gap because it gives "verifiable onchain attestations rather than just warnings."
   - **ShieldBot** (3rd place): intercepts risky transactions in real time, blocks dangerous contracts pre-execution.
   - **Aegis Protocol** (6th place): autonomous agent continuously monitoring DeFi positions, LLM reasoning + onchain DEX data, executes "verifiable protective actions."
   Source: [Good Vibes Only: OpenClaw Edition — The Winners](https://www.bnbchain.org/en/blog/good-vibes-only-openclaw-edition-winners), corroborated by [BNB Chain OpenClaw Hackathon Awards $100K to 10 AI Agent Projects](https://blockchain.news/news/bnb-chain-openclaw-hackathon-awards-100k-ai-agent-projects). No direct judge quotes were recoverable from the source explaining *why* these specific projects scored where they did, beyond the "verifiable onchain attestation vs. just warnings" framing for VibeCheck — this specific point should be treated as the strongest available analog but not over-interpreted as a full rubric.
2. **Second precedent**: Chainlink's Convergence Hackathon produced "CRE Risk Router," described as an onchain risk decision layer for autonomous DeFi agents — it evaluates trades before execution via logic-gate risk checks, then **writes an onchain attestation** indicating approved/constrained/rejected. Source: search-result summary referencing [Chainlink Convergence Hackathon Winners](https://news.blockreq.com/en/news/chainlink-convergence-hackathon-winners-2026/) (the source page itself returned a server error on fetch attempt — this is a secondary citation via search snippet, not independently verified in full).
3. ETHGlobal's dedicated AI-agent event ("Agentic Ethereum") scores on Technicality/Originality/Practicality/Usability/WOW, with demo videos capped at 2–4 minutes and explicit judge guidance to "show the project functioning" rather than describe it — no agent-specific demo template was published beyond general video-quality requirements (audio, 720p minimum). Source: [ETHGlobal Agentic Ethereum details](https://ethglobal.com/events/agents/info/details).
4. A structural observation from the "8,200 projects" analysis: AI-agent submissions as a category went from ~1% to over a fifth of all ETHGlobal submissions across recent events, and these spikes correlate directly with sponsor/track emphasis (e.g., a dedicated Agentic Ethereum event) rather than organic growth — meaning **judges at agent-themed or agent-friendly events have seen a large and rising volume of agent submissions**, raising the bar for what counts as differentiated within the category. Source: [What 8,200 hackathon projects reveal about what actually wins](https://simbro.medium.com/what-8-200-hackathon-projects-reveal-about-what-actually-wins-f105346ec97c) (secondary, same caveat as section 2.2).
5. (inference) Combining #1, #2, and #4: risk-scoring + onchain-attestation + agent-facing submissions are a recognized, already-rewarded pattern (not a novel category to be explained from scratch to judges), but the category is getting crowded — differentiation likely has to come from *deterministic, auditable scoring logic* (as opposed to opaque LLM-only scoring) and from *evidence of actually being usable by an agent* (a real MCP tool call an agent could invoke, not just a described API), since none of the three BNB Chain precedents advertise a non-LLM deterministic scoring core — that would be a distinguishing feature if it holds up under judge scrutiny.
6. No source discussed *video* production specifics for agent-workflow demos beyond generic length/quality requirements (see #3) — the "how do winners demo agent workflows convincingly" question is only answered indirectly: via the "show it functioning, one flawless workflow" principle from section 2, applied to whatever the agent does. No agent-hackathon-specific video breakdown (e.g., screen layout, voiceover pacing) was found.

## Top 10 leverage points for an infra submission in THIS hackathon

Ranked by expected impact, given the project is a risk-scoring layer + MCP server + on-chain attestation registry + SDK feedback report for DreamDEX event contracts on Somnia:

1. **Make the MCP tool call itself the demo's spine, not a described feature.** DreamDEX's own architecture pitch is "agent-first, MCP-native" (§1.4) — a submission that shows a real MCP tool invocation scoring a live DreamDEX market is the single highest-leverage alignment available, because it matches the sponsor's stated identity rather than asking judges to extrapolate fit.
2. **Lead the demo with one flawless end-to-end flow** (market → risk score → on-chain attestation write → agent reads attestation before acting), not a tour of five components. This is the most consistently repeated finding across every source in §2 and §5.
3. **Put the "can I check this myself" affordance front and center**: a single command or script judges can run to reproduce the score/attestation, per §3.5 and the reproducibility criteria in §3.1. This is cheap to build and directly targets the credibility gap every source flags.
4. **Report validation numbers narrowly and honestly, with method attached inline** — sample size, run count, what's *not* covered — rather than a headline percentage. §3.1–3.3 show this is what separates trusted from distrusted claims, and precision-without-scope is actively a red flag to a skeptical reader.
5. **Frame the deterministic, non-LLM scoring core as the differentiator against the closest precedent set** (VibeCheck/ShieldBot/Aegis Protocol, §5.1, §5.5) — none of those advertise auditable non-LLM logic; if LevelField's scoring is genuinely deterministic/code-verified, say so explicitly and contrast it, since judges at agent-themed events have seen many LLM-scored risk tools already (§5.4).
6. **Build the SDK feedback report as a Ledger-style checklist**, not prose: concrete gap → quoted evidence (the exact failing call/response) → concrete suggested fix. §4.1–4.4 is the only sourced format signal for this deliverable, and it rewards specificity over volume.
7. **Show the on-chain attestation on a block explorer**, not just in a script's stdout — this is the cheapest possible "run it yourself" upgrade and mirrors VibeCheck's stated differentiator ("verifiable onchain attestations rather than just warnings," §5.1).
8. **State problem-first, in one sentence, before any architecture** — JetBrains' #1 failure mode is skipping this, and it's free to fix (§2.3–2.4).
9. **Do not present anything live/flaky as core to the pitch** — mock or pre-fill anything that isn't the actual differentiator (indexer calls, market data feeds), per the explicit "if you're going to mock part of your project, mock things that aren't core" guidance surfaced in research (§2, browser-use source) and the "unpolished demo" failure mode (§2.4).
10. **Treat the Somnia/DreamDEX-specific rubric as unconfirmed and hedge accordingly**: since no public rubric for this exact hackathon was found (top of memo, §1.6), default to the union of what's consistently rewarded across Somnia's own mini-hackathons (technical implementation, effective use of the platform's native features — Reactivity/MCP — integration depth, and production/ecosystem potential, §1.2) rather than assuming an ETHGlobal-style multi-track rubric applies verbatim.
