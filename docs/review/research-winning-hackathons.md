# Research memo: what distinguishes winning web3/crypto hackathon submissions

Date: 2026-08-20
Scope: ETHGlobal (finalists/prizes), DoraHacks BUIDL, Chainlink hackathons, Solana/Colosseum, Encode Club, plus Devpost judge commentary (Devpost hosts many crypto hackathons including Chainlink's). Web research only, ~25 fetches. Claims without a source are marked (inference).

---

## 1. Demo video patterns

- **Length is a hard gate, not a suggestion.** ETHGlobal: videos "must be between 2 and 4 minutes" and are "automatically rejected during upload" outside that range. Colosseum requires a separate pitch video capped at 3 minutes plus a technical demo video. — [ETHGlobal New York 2025 details](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/details), [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon)
- **Judges' real attention budget is under a minute.** Colosseum: "Judges spend 3-5 minutes per project. If they can't understand what you built in 60 seconds, you lose." — [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon)
- **First seconds are a hook, not a bio.** ETHGlobal caps team/backstory intros at "no more than 20 seconds"; general hackathon-pitch guidance (Nader Dabit, ex-Edge & Node) frames the opening 15-20 seconds as "Hook them" with a powerful statement or question, before any solution explanation. — [ETHGlobal details](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/details), [Nader Dabit pitch gist](https://gist.github.com/dabit3/caef5eee4753dd7d23767bc31e70da28)
- **Live product dominates screen time; slides are a minor accessory.** Devpost: "Keep it short, interesting (no slide shows/decks), and visually engaging... Show a demo of the product running by using some input." ETHGlobal allows slides only to summarize points, "no more than 4 bullet points per slide." — [Devpost demo video tips](https://info.devpost.com/blog/6-tips-for-making-a-hackathon-demo-video), [ETHGlobal details](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/details)
- **"Proof it works" = edited-but-real footage, not staged claims.** ETHGlobal instructs cutting dead time ("skip any unnecessary waiting" like MetaMask confirmations) but explicitly bans AI voiceover/TTS narration, phone recordings, sub-720p footage, and artificial speed-up to fit the time limit — i.e., editing for pace is fine, faking the interaction is not. Nader Dabit's guide independently recommends pre-recording and editing a live demo (QuickTime/Camtasia) rather than risking a live-coding failure on stage. — [ETHGlobal details](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/details), [Nader Dabit pitch gist](https://gist.github.com/dabit3/caef5eee4753dd7d23767bc31e70da28)
- **Infra fragility during the demo is treated as a fatal risk, not a footnote.** Colosseum guide: "Localhost demos fail. Network issues happen. Deploy early, deploy often" (deploy to devnet, not localhost) and integrate priority-fee estimation because "Your demo transaction hangs during judging = death." — [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon)

## 2. Submission page / README structure

- **A fixed "complete artifact set" recurs across ecosystems, and any missing piece is treated as a red flag.** HackQuest: submissions need "the contract address, Scan URL, demo video, GitHub link, and live link (if available)"; Colosseum requires a GitHub repo built during the event, a pitch video, and a technical demo video (weekly update videos are optional but "increase visibility with judges"); DoraHacks BUIDLs must include working demo/demo-link URLs with `http/https://` before an organizer will even approve the listing. — [HackQuest best practices](https://www.hackquest.io/blog/Best-Practices-for-Successful-Web3-Hackathon-Project-Submissions), [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon), [DoraHacks BUIDL submission guide](https://dorahacks.io/blog/dora-grant-dao/)
- **Deployed, verifiable on-chain state beats a description of what the chain interaction would look like.** HackQuest lists "failing to deploy your smart contract on a Testnet or not providing the Scan URL" and deploying "on the wrong blockchain" as concrete disqualifiers, alongside "broken code, non-functional contracts, or incomplete features." — [HackQuest best practices](https://www.hackquest.io/blog/Best-Practices-for-Successful-Web3-Hackathon-Project-Submissions)
- **Above the fold: tagline, live demo + source links, then a component-by-component build breakdown.** An actual ETHGlobal showcase page places the tagline and "Live Demo" / "Source Code" buttons immediately below the title, then a description, then a "How it's made" section that splits the architecture by component (e.g., backend data pipeline vs. frontend) rather than a single undifferentiated tech-stack list. — [ETHGlobal Explorer showcase page](https://ethglobal.com/showcase/ethglobal-explorer-i8ie4)
- **Commit history has to visibly show incremental build-up, not one dump.** The Bitcoin.com guide to winning web3 hackathons: "a repository must not contain a single commit with thousands of lines, but rather have a commit history with a gradual buildup of the project" — this both proves the "built during the event" claim and functions as evidence judges/organizers check. — [The Definitive Guide to Winning Web3 Hackathons](https://news.bitcoin.com/the-definitive-guide-to-winning-web3-hackathons/)
- **Inaccessible links are cited independently by multiple judges/organizers as a top disqualifier**, not just a minor deduction — Devpost's own guidance: "you'd be surprised how many submissions are disqualified simply because they didn't meet the baseline criteria," specifically calling out broken or private demo links. — [Devpost: understanding submission and judging criteria](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria)
- **Duplicate/near-duplicate resubmissions across categories are explicitly penalized**, so winners tailor one complete submission per track rather than reusing a shell across multiple bounties. — [HackQuest best practices](https://www.hackquest.io/blog/Best-Practices-for-Successful-Web3-Hackathon-Project-Submissions)

## 3. Judging criteria as actually applied

- **ETHGlobal's published rubric has five named axes**: Technicality, Originality, Practicality, Usability (UI/UX/DX), and "WOW Factor" — a distinct fifth axis for memorability beyond the other four, applied at partner-judged and finalist review. — [ETHGlobal New York 2025 details](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/details)
- **Delivery outweighs ambition in point-weighted rubrics.** Algorand Foundation's guide gives "a functioning MVP" 40 of 100 points — the single largest weight, ahead of market viability (20) and presentation (10) — explicitly framed as "emphasizing delivery over ambition." — [Algorand: how to win a web3 hackathon](https://algorand.co/blog/how-to-win-web3-hackathon-survival-guide)
- **Named judges converge on the same instant-fail pattern: not meeting the stated baseline requirements at all.** Devpost judge quotes: Karen (Databricks) was "surprised to see how many submissions did not fulfill the basic requirements"; Richard (Square) flags submissions that "shoehorn" an unrelated idea into the contest instead of addressing the prompt; Kelvin (Google) penalizes projects that just modify a template without creative departure; Warren (Atlassian) — "It's not sufficient to just build the app and have it out there," the pitch/explanation needs equal effort to the build. — [Devpost: how to win a hackathon, advice from 5 judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)
- **"Start built during the event" is a hard eligibility gate, not a criterion judges merely weigh.** ETHGlobal's "Start Fresh"/Classic track: "Your project must be started and developed during the hackathon" — pre-existing project-specific code, designs, or assets disqualify a project from that track's finalist/partner-prize consideration (public libraries/boilerplate remain allowed). A newer rule also strips partner-prize eligibility from AI-generated projects "lacking meaningful team contributions." — [ETHGlobal New York 2025 start info](https://ethglobal.com/events/newyork2025/info/start)
- **DoraHacks-style rubrics are organizer-configured, but the pattern is 3-4 criteria weighted toward the hackathon's actual goal** — e.g., an SDK-adoption hackathon can weight "correct implementation" at 40% versus "good presentation" at only 10%, meaning what "wins" is contingent on what the specific sponsor is optimizing for, not a universal formula. — [DoraHacks: how to design a hackathon judging plan](https://dorahacks.io/blog/guides/hackathon-judging-plan)
- **Chain/network-eligibility mismatches are a recurring, avoidable instant-fail**: deploying to the wrong blockchain, or failing to deploy to a recognized testnet/mainnet with a verifiable explorer link, disqualifies otherwise-complete projects. — [HackQuest best practices](https://www.hackquest.io/blog/Best-Practices-for-Successful-Web3-Hackathon-Project-Submissions)

## 4. Scope presentation

- **The dominant advice is "narrow and complete" over "broad and half-built," stated almost identically across ecosystems.** Colosseum guide contrasts explicitly: "Let's build a full DEX with limit orders, margin trading, and governance" (wrong) vs. "Let's build one innovative swap mechanism and nail the UX" (right); its stated formula is "Working demo > perfect architecture" and "Clear problem statement > impressive tech." The Bitcoin.com web3-hackathon guide states the same principle independently: "A clean working demo of one thing beats a half-built demo of five things, as judges do not have time to imagine what you meant." — [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon), [The Definitive Guide to Winning Web3 Hackathons](https://news.bitcoin.com/the-definitive-guide-to-winning-web3-hackathons/)
- **Sponsor-integration hackathons can reward more surface area, as long as every added surface actually works.** Chainlink's Block Magic winners did not stay minimal: the grand-prize winner built a "seven-level educational progression" wiring together four separate Chainlink services (Automation, Functions, VRF, CCIP) across distinct gameplay mechanics, and 45% of all participants used multiple Chainlink services. The organizing narrative frames this as a "complete user experience," not sprawl — the distinguishing factor is that every added component was functional and integrated, not merely declared. — [Chainlink Block Magic winners](https://chain.link/blog/block-magic-winners)
- **Judges explicitly detect and penalize scope that doesn't match the stated prompt/track**, independent of build quality — Devpost's Richard (Square): flags submissions that "shoehorn" an idea into a track rather than genuinely addressing it; Warren (Atlassian): if the prompt is about a specific use case, "really show off that use case" rather than spreading effort evenly across unrelated features. — [Devpost: how to win a hackathon, advice from 5 judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)
- **Originality is explicitly framed as "the non-obvious angle," and the obvious idea is treated as a signal to restart, not refine.** Bitcoin.com guide: "The idea has to be uniquely yours, not the obvious one... if your idea is the first thing anyone would think of, you should kill it and start over." — [The Definitive Guide to Winning Web3 Hackathons](https://news.bitcoin.com/the-definitive-guide-to-winning-web3-hackathons/)
- **Honest handling of limitations wasn't found stated as its own rubric line item in any source fetched.** The closest analog is Nader Dabit's advice to prepare "backup slides for detailed information" so a team can address skepticism about gaps without derailing the core pitch — i.e., limitations get a rehearsed, contained answer rather than a hidden or over-apologized one. (inference: this is the live-pitch equivalent of a README "Known Limitations" section, not a direct quote about one) — [Nader Dabit pitch gist](https://gist.github.com/dabit3/caef5eee4753dd7d23767bc31e70da28)

## 5. Ecosystem-fit signals

- **Using the sponsor's own official tooling — instead of reinventing infrastructure — is explicitly rewarded, and self-built infra is explicitly discouraged.** Colosseum: "Don't write your own wallet adapter. Don't build your own RPC infrastructure. Use `create-solana-dapp` and Helius," alongside judging for "smart use of Solana's strengths (speed, low cost, composability)." — [Colosseum hackathon guide](https://github.com/SuperteamCanada/how-to-win-colosseum-hackathon)
- **Depth of native integration (multiple services, meaningfully combined) beats a single superficial API call.** Chainlink's Block Magic winners across categories stacked 2-4 distinct Chainlink services rather than calling one function once; financial-category winners combined data feeds with cross-chain (CCIP) capability specifically. — [Chainlink Block Magic winners](https://chain.link/blog/block-magic-winners)
- **Feedback to the sponsor is a scored rubric item for partner prizes, not a courtesy add-on.** ETHGlobal's partner-prize judging explicitly requires teams to "explain how you've used or integrated their tools, provide feedback, and share relevant comments" as part of what's evaluated. — [The Definitive Guide to Winning Web3 Hackathons](https://news.bitcoin.com/the-definitive-guide-to-winning-web3-hackathons/) (paraphrasing ETHGlobal partner-prize process)
- **Early, proactive sponsor engagement during the event (not just at submission) is repeatedly named as a differentiator.** "The best strategy is to approach potential sponsors as early as possible during the event. Don't wait until the end; start conversations right away." A first-hand ETHGlobal winner account corroborates this at the tactical level: deliberately building toward specific sponsor tracks and integrating multiple sponsor SDKs (Moralis, Uniswap v3, Web3Auth) won three separate prizes for one project. — [The Definitive Guide to Winning Web3 Hackathons](https://news.bitcoin.com/the-definitive-guide-to-winning-web3-hackathons/), [James Bachini: EthGlobal hackathon, winning $4300](https://jamesbachini.com/ethglobal-hackathon/)
- **A large-sample correlational analysis suggests chasing that specific event's sponsor theme predicts what wins more strongly than generic technical merit.** An analysis of ~8,200 public ETHGlobal showcase projects across 38 events found submission-theme share swings tightly track sponsor/prize themes of each specific event — e.g., AI-agent submissions jumped from roughly 1% to over 20% of projects coinciding with the dedicated "Agentic Ethereum" event, and cross-chain submissions spiked alongside the 1inch-sponsored "Unite DeFi" event. The author's framing: these spikes are "somewhat manufactured" by sponsor incentives rather than organic developer trends. (inference: correlation between submission volume and prize track ≠ direct evidence of individual judging outcomes, but it is a strong ecosystem-level signal that projects aligned with the event's specific sponsor theme concentrate where the money is, and by extension where the prizes are) — [Simon Brown: what 8,200 hackathon projects reveal about what actually wins](https://simbro.medium.com/what-8-200-hackathon-projects-reveal-about-what-actually-wins-f105346ec97c)
- **Building from a sponsor's own reference/example code is treated as a legitimate accelerant, not a shortcut penalty.** Algorand's guide directs participants to the DevRel team's own GitHub examples repo as a starting point for "a clear Algorand protocol implementation." — [Algorand: how to win a web3 hackathon](https://algorand.co/blog/how-to-win-web3-hackathon-survival-guide)

## What a submission must nail, in order

1. Ship a working, on-chain-verifiable artifact (deployed to the correct testnet/mainnet, with a public explorer/scan URL and a live link) — broken or missing links are the single most repeated instant-fail across every source reviewed.
2. Build the whole thing during the event window with a visibly incremental commit history; pre-existing code disqualifies "from scratch"/Classic-track entries and undermines credibility even where allowed.
3. Hit the exact demo-video length window (commonly 2-4 minutes) — several platforms auto-reject outside it before a judge ever watches.
4. Open the video with a hook in the first 15-20 seconds (problem statement, stat, or question); cap team/backstory intro at ~20 seconds.
5. Spend the bulk of the video on the live product actually running with real input; keep slides minimal (≤4 bullets each) and cut dead/waiting time without faking the interaction.
6. Scope to one thing that fully works over several things half-built, unless the hackathon explicitly rewards a broader "complete experience" (e.g., multi-service sponsor-integration formats) — in that case every added surface must still be fully functional, not just declared.
7. Go deep and native on one sponsor's SDK/chain — use their own tooling instead of reinventing infrastructure, and combine multiple of their services meaningfully rather than a single superficial call.
8. Put deployed links, contract address, and repo above the fold on the submission page, followed by a component-by-component "how it's made" breakdown judges can skim in seconds.
9. Explicitly map the submission to the hackathon's own published judging criteria and to the specific track/prompt — judges detect and penalize scope that's shoehorned in rather than aligned.
10. For sponsor/partner bounty prizes, write explicit integration notes and feedback about the sponsor's tool — this is graded, not optional politeness.
11. Engage sponsor mentors/Discord early in the event (not just at submission); it both unblocks technical problems and signals genuine ecosystem engagement before judging starts.
12. Rehearse the pitch to survive a judge's compressed attention (3-5 minutes total, understandable within the first 60 seconds), with prepared, contained answers for known limitations rather than hidden or over-apologized gaps.
